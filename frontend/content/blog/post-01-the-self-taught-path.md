# The Self-Taught Path: Why Music Production and Sanctuary Sound Matter

*I never had lessons. I had a dusty guitar, a phone full of saved videos and a church that needed help.*

![A guitarist in a green t-shirt playing a white electric guitar with a strap, seen from the chest down](/images/blog/guitar_3.jpg)
*Figure 1. Playing my white Squier Stratocaster.*

## A guitar that was only decoration

In 2020, when COVID locked everyone inside, my Instagram feed slowly filled up with guitar videos. People playing riffs, solos, chord tricks. I was always fascinated by people who were *really* good at guitar, and the more I watched, the more I wanted to understand what they were doing.

The funny thing is, I already had a guitar. It was an old Spanish acoustic standing in my room, and it was pure decoration. I had never really played it.

One day, stuck at home with nothing to do, I asked myself a simple question: *why don't I just play it?*

> So I did. And I was bad. Really bad.

That old acoustic had a very high string action, which means the strings sit far above the fretboard and you have to press hard to get a clean note. It made everything harder. But looking back, it was also a hidden advantage: it built up my finger strength fast.

![A dark acoustic guitar with a cutaway body, lit by a single beam of light against a black background](/images/blog/stock-acoustic-guitar.webp)
*Photo by [Harshveen Kalsi](https://unsplash.com/photos/a-close-up-of-an-acoustic-guitar-on-a-black-background-kyCFXYIO0LA), Unsplash.*

## Learning from my phone

Because almost every video I watched was electric guitar, I naturally wanted one. For my birthday in May I asked for an electric guitar and got a white Squier Stratocaster-style guitar with an HSS pickup layout. It was cheap, but to me it was precious. After watching all those players online, I finally had my own.

My "teacher" was Instagram. I built a saved collection of videos and posts about music theory, techniques and players I liked, so I could go back and review them whenever I wanted. Because I kept returning to the same lessons, music theory started to feel surprisingly easy.

It turns out I'm not unusual. Researchers have described how young musicians are moving from the classic "listen and copy" way of learning to ["watching and copying" through online videos](https://doi.org/10.5429/2079-3871(2023)v13i3.7en), and argue that this is a real form of informal learning. That matched my experience exactly.

## From the bedroom to the stage

After one to two years of playing, people started noticing that I'd gotten pretty good. Then someone at my church asked if I wanted to play during the service.

I hesitated. Until then I had only ever played in my bedroom, and a stage, even a small one, felt scary. I said yes anyway. And honestly? My first time didn't go as well as I'd hoped. *I'll tell that story properly in [INTERNAL LINK: Post 04, "First Time Playing at the Sanctuary"].*

## Then someone handed me the mixer

Once people knew I played guitar *and* recorded at home, they assumed I could also run sound. That wasn't entirely wrong, because recording at home had given me some confidence. So when I was asked to take over the mixer, I agreed.

Then I stood in front of it and saw what felt like a thousand buttons. I had no idea what most of them did.

![A large digital live sound console with touchscreens, rotary knobs and faders in blue light](/images/blog/stock-live-console.webp)
*Photo by [Ayanangshu Biswas](https://unsplash.com/photos/a-professional-audio-mixing-console-with-blue-lighting-fYheZxcB3nM), Unsplash.*

I learned the way I learned guitar: trial and error, plus someone showing me the basics. After that I went deeper on YouTube, watching people explain how to work more efficiently, what EQ and compression actually do, and why proper gain staging matters. *Gain staging* means setting a healthy signal level at every step of the chain, so you keep enough headroom without burying the sound in noise ([Sound On Sound explains it well here](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/gain-staging-your-daw-software)). Every service behind the desk made it a bit clearer. *Want the step-by-step version? Read [INTERNAL LINK: Post 03, "Sunday Morning Panic: Mixing Unfamiliar Consoles"].*

## Why home recording made live sound easier

This is the part I think most people miss:

> **the bedroom and the sanctuary are not two separate worlds.**

![Close-up of a mixing console with faders, mute buttons and colored lights](/images/blog/stock-mixer-faders.webp)
*Photo by [Seungmin Yoon](https://unsplash.com/photos/close-up-of-a-professional-audio-mixing-console-with-sliders-qxx-YvGxvOk), Unsplash.*

My home setup was simple. I started with a Mooer Hornet amp with built-in effects, plugged straight into my guitar. It worked, but it never gave me the sound I was looking for. So I bought my first audio interface, the Roland Duo-Capture mk2, and started recording in [Cakewalk by BandLab](https://www.bandlab.com/products/cakewalk), a free DAW (digital audio workstation). With the interface I could use amp modelers and VST plugins and get far more sounds than my amp could give me.

It wasn't the best interface. It was the best I could afford. It came with noticeable latency and a low-frequency hum, and I didn't even know noise gates existed. A [noise gate](https://producelikeapro.com/blog/noise-gate/) mutes the signal when it drops below a threshold, which is exactly what you need when a guitar hums between phrases. Learning that the hard way was frustrating, but it taught me a lot.

All that messing around with EQ and levels at home meant that when I stood behind the church mixer, the core principles weren't new.

> The buttons were. The ideas weren't.

## My take: get thrown in the deep end

Here's what I believe after all this:

> **people learn far more when they're thrown into the deep end than when they dabble in and out.**

That goes for guitar and for mixing.

I'm not against formal training. Some structure helps, and I wish I'd had more of it at the start. But I think churches, and anyone teaching audio, should give learners real responsibility early. Put them on the stage. Put them behind the desk. That pressure, with a bit of guidance, is what made me learn fast.

## Key takeaways

- **You don't need a perfect start.** A badly set-up acoustic and a cheap interface taught me more than I expected.
- **Collect and repeat.** Saving and re-watching lessons made theory stick.
- **Home skills transfer.** EQ and gain basics from the bedroom carry straight over to live sound.
- **Say yes before you feel ready.** Responsibility early speeds up learning, even if the first try goes wrong.
