# First Time Playing at the Sanctuary

*Playing in your bedroom and playing in a worship team are two completely different skills. I learned that the hard way. This is the story of my first sets, the feedback that changed everything, and the setup I use now.*

![A man in a white polo with an in-ear monitor sits on a chair playing a white electric guitar in front of a dark blue curtain](/images/blog/guitar_portrait_1.webp)
*Figure 1. Photo by the author.*

## My first set: eyes on the fretboard

About two years ago, I played in my local church for the first time. I played acoustic guitar. I could play electric, but I wasn't confident enough yet to play it in front of the whole congregation. It was just me and a keyboard player. *(How I got to that point is in [The Self-Taught Path](/blog/self-taught-path).)*

The music was **mezmur**, worship songs from the Ethiopian and Eritrean tradition. It has its own feel, built a lot around minor pentatonic melodies and the chords that go with them. *Ethiopian music in general is built on pentatonic modes called qenet, which researchers have studied in detail ([Weisser & Falceto, 2013](https://doi.org/10.3406/ethio.2013.1539))*

In my bedroom I mostly played melodies along with songs. In church, everything was different:

- **I didn't know how to blend with the keys.** The keyboard was already playing melodies *and* chords. Where was my space?
- **I never looked up.** We had no in-ears, so the whole band followed the worship leader's cues by watching her. But my eyes were glued to my fretboard. I missed cues and kept playing the chorus when the song had already moved on.
- **I didn't know which chords to play.** I had never learned which chords fit what the singer is doing.

Afterwards, everyone told me it went well. But that wasn't what I wanted to hear. I wanted honest feedback, ideally from someone with musical knowledge who could tell me where to go next.

## The second church: "something is missing"

A few months later, I started serving at a bigger church with a youth congregation. They played mostly English worship songs, like Hillsong and Elevation Worship, a completely different style. And this time I brought my electric guitar.

New problems showed up straight away.

**My tone was bland.** I had no pedals, so my guitar went straight into the PA. The sound team added some reverb and delay on the X32 (I now know what that job looks like from the other side, see [Sunday Morning Panic](/blog/mixing-unfamiliar-church-sound-consoles)), but it still sounded nothing like my home setup, where I had a laptop full of plugins to shape my sound. *(That home setup is in [The €150 Home Studio](/blog/budget-home-studio-recording-gear-guitarists).)*

**I didn't know what to play.** There was no other electric guitarist at either church, so I had to figure it out alone. On YouTube, worship guitarists always seemed to know exactly what they were doing. I mostly copied the vocal melody, which got repetitive fast.

**My timing was off.** I almost never practiced with a metronome, so I missed beats, chord tones and transitions from verse to chorus.

**I had to play rhythm and lead at once.** At home I mostly played chords. Now I had to switch constantly: melodic lines in the chorus, power chords to drive the groove in the verse, swells in the slow songs. I didn't know any of that yet.

In every feedback session, I heard the same thing: *"It sounded good, but something was lacking."* Nobody could tell me what.

## The feedback that changed everything

One day a guest musician came by. He served at another church and knew several instruments, including electric guitar. He gave the whole worship team feedback, and what he told me was simple but huge: **be dynamic in everything you play.**

He also told the rest of the team to support me as best they could, since I was the only electric guitarist.

So I made a decision: I stepped back from the worship team for a while to work on my playing.

## What I practiced (and what finally worked)

For a few months I focused on worship guitar specifically:

- **Hitting chord tones exactly when the chord changes**Instead of just copying the melody.
- **Techniques for worship guitar:** Swells, palm muting, power-chord strumming.
- **Matching the song's energy.** Upbeat songs got palm-muted downstrokes and almost no swells. Slow songs got soft swells that follow the melody.
- **Call and response with the keyboard player** One plays a line, the other answers.
- **Filling the spaces.** This was the big one. A small lick in a gap adds flair, "a bit of sauce", and I think this was exactly what everyone said was missing.
- **Not depending on one key.** Worship leaders sometimes change key last minute. The keyboard player just presses transpose, but the bassist and I have to be ready.
- **Thinking in numbers instead of chord names** So a key change doesn't throw me off. This is called the [Nashville Number System](https://www.sweetwater.com/insync/nashville-number-system/). In the key of G, for example, G-C-D is just 1-4-5.

I didn't go deep into modes. Major and minor were enough for me. But knowing my way around the fretboard helped me find chord tones and inversions that sound a bit more unique.

**My teachers were still Instagram and YouTube.** I know many people avoid social media for learning because there's so much information, and a lot of it isn't reliable. I agree. But if you find the right creators, save their posts and practice them, you get short, focused lessons that would otherwise be buried in a 40-minute video.

When I came back to the team, people noticed. For the first time, the feedback was that something had *improved*.



## Reading the room

Being dynamic also means watching the people around you. If the worship leader raises her voice a little, then a little more, we're probably heading into the bridge, where everything builds up. I need to build with it.

I could never have learned that in my bedroom. Playing along with songs I liked was a good stepping stone, but a worship team needs something different.

## Chasing the tone: my €75 multi-effects pedal

The tone problem was still there. On worship videos I kept seeing guitarists stepping on huge pedalboards that probably cost hundreds or thousands. Honestly, it intimidated me. I thought I'd never get the sound I wanted.

Then I found **multi-effects pedals**: one small unit that does amps, drives, delays, reverbs and more. The famous ones, like the Neural DSP Quad Cortex, Line 6 Helix and Fractal FM9, cost far more than I could spend. But there were budget options too, like the NUX MG-30, Mooer GE100 and **Valeton GP-100**.

I found a GP-100 on Marktplaats for €75 and bought it immediately. I still use it today at both churches, in the studio with friends, and on other stages. It's small, the metal body is tough, and it has a built-in expression pedal ([GP-100 specs](https://www.thomann.co.uk/valeton_gp_100.htm)).

The catch: it only has **two footswitches**. So instead of dozens of sounds, I keep a few presets I can switch between fast:

| Preset | What it's for |
|---|---|
| **Edge of breakup** | My bread and butter. With the guitar volume around 3 on the neck pickup it's clean; turn it up to around 7 on the bridge and it breaks up and cuts through the mix in the chorus. |
| **Jazz clean** | For mezmur songs. I can switch effects on and off inside the preset, e.g. chorus in the verse, overdrive in the bridge. |
| **Standard clean** | Clean with reverb and delay. Like edge of breakup, but cleaner, with no grit. |
| **Solo** | Plenty of overdrive and gain, but not harsh. Inspired by players like Kitamura Ken and Misha Mansoor. |
| **Bonus: Funk** | Using the expression pedal as a wah. |

Those few presets cover almost everything I need. Someday I might upgrade to the GP-200 for more footswitches and a bigger screen, but for now the GP-100 is plenty. And because I already understood how to chase a tone from home recording ([The €150 Home Studio](/blog/budget-home-studio-recording-gear-guitarists)), setting it up was easy.

## How I prepare for a set now

1. **Numbers, not letters.** I look at the chord chart as numbers, so the key doesn't matter.
2. **Chord tones per number.** I find the chord tones and a groove or melody that isn't the vocal line.
3. **Genre first.** I think about what fits the style. For upbeat songs, I focus on rhythm and chords that just fit.
4.  **A metronome.** Practicing with a metronome is key if you want to stay on beat and building that internal rhythm in you.

## My advice before your first worship set

- **Be dynamic.** In volume, in style, and in what you choose *not* to play.
- **Look up.** Watch the worship leader and the band, not your fretboard.
- **Learn the number system** So key changes don't scare you.
- **Get a setup that switches styles easily.** A budget multi-effects pedal is enough.
- **Broaden your music taste** So you already have ideas for whatever style you're asked to play.
- **Ask for honest feedback** And if you need to, step back to practice. It's worth it.

## Key takeaways

- Bedroom playing and band playing are different skills.
- "Sounds good, but something's missing" usually means **dynamics and space**.
- Numbers make key changes manageable.
- A €75 multi-effects pedal with four good presets beats a giant board you can't afford.
