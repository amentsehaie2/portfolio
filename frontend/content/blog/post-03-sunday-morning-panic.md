# Sunday Morning Panic: Mixing Unfamiliar Consoles

*The first time I stood behind a church mixer, I didn't know what a monitor was. Three years and three consoles later, here's what I've learned, including what to do when everything goes wrong in the middle of a service.*

![A man wearing headphones and a light blue shirt adjusts the faders of an Allen & Heath Qu mixer, with two people working at laptops behind him](/images/blog/mixer-front-of-house.webp)
*Figure 1. Behind the Allen & Heath Qu mixer.*

## A wall of buttons and a phone call

When I was asked to run sound for the first time, I hesitated, then said yes. The mixer was a **Behringer Xenyx X2222USB**, a 22-input analog desk. I looked at all those knobs and had no idea where to start.

So I called a friend. I explained what I knew and, more importantly, what I didn't. He calmly told me what to focus on and what to ignore for now:

- **Gain staging:** set a healthy input level on every channel first.
- **Faders:** where the volume of each channel should roughly sit.
- **Basic EQ:** nothing fancy yet. He knew I wouldn't understand the details.
- **Cables:** quarter-inch (jack), XLR, MIDI and power cables, and what a **snake** is, one thick cable that carries all the stage channels back to the mixer. I had no idea that existed.

Then people started walking up to me during the service: *"Can you put me louder in the monitor?"* I didn't know what a monitor was. Later I called my friend again, and he explained there are two kinds of output: **front of house** (what the congregation hears) and **monitors** (separate speakers so singers and musicians can hear themselves on stage).

That was a big lesson: **everyone in the room hears a different mix**, and part of my job is to look after all of them.

## Three consoles, three learning curves

Over about three years I've worked on three different desks across two churches.

| Console | Type | What it taught me |
|---|---|---|
| Behringer Xenyx X2222USB | Analog | The basics, the hard way |
| Behringer X32 | Digital | Seeing what my EQ, gate and compressor actually do |
| Allen & Heath Qu-6D | Digital | Monitor and in-ear mixes that finally made sense |

### Analog: mixing blind

The analog Xenyx confused me the most. Coming from home recording, I was used to *seeing* EQ curves in my DAW. On an analog desk there's nothing to look at. You turn a knob and have to imagine what it's doing to the sound.

### The X32: finally, something to see

When my church switched to a **Behringer X32**, things got much easier. I could see the gate, the compressor and the EQ curve on screen, just like in my DAW. We also moved from the old snake to a **digital stage box**, connected to the desk with a single network cable ([Sweetwater explains the X32 stage-box setup](https://www.sweetwater.com/sweetcare/articles/behringer-x32-s16-setup/)). Seeing every input and output in one place made the whole system click for me.

What still felt hard was the monitor mixes. I had to go through the sends channel by channel to build each musician's mix. 

### The Qu-6D: a dream come true

My friend later asked me to help on the sound team at his church. They had also started on an analog Behringer and then moved to an **Allen & Heath Qu-6D**. 

The Qu has dedicated buttons for each mix. Press one, and all the faders move to show that person's mix. I'd only ever seen motorized faders fly around in Instagram videos, so the first time I saw it in real life I couldn't believe my eyes.

![A man wearing headphones and a light blue shirt touches the faders of an Allen & Heath Qu mixer](/images/blog/mixer-qu-portrait.webp "portrait")
*Figure 2. Working the faders on the Qu.*

That church also started using **subs and tops** and **in-ear monitors**. The idea that every musician could have their own mix in their own ears, hearing only what they want, amazed me.

## My Sunday routine now

Both churches set up from scratch every time, so a service starts long before anyone sings.

1. **Load in.** We carry the mixer, subs, tops, monitors and stage box from the storage room into the church.
2. **Cabling.** Two other volunteers plug in the instruments, mics and the network cable between stage box and desk.
3. **Prep the desk.** I set the gain on every channel and load template EQ curves per person and instrument, a starting point to adjust from.
4. **Vocals, one by one.** Each singer sings solo so I can fix their channel.
5. **Vocals together.** I listen to the harmony and for frequencies that bounce off the walls and start to ring. That ringing is the start of feedback, which is what we want to avoid (more in [INTERNAL LINK: Post 06, "Eliminating Live Vocal Feedback"]).
6. **Instruments, one by one.** Electric guitar, acoustic guitar, drums, and the saxophone when he plays.
7. **Instruments together, then the full band.**
8. **Ask the team.** After rehearsal, I ask if anyone needs anything in their monitor or in-ears, and we fix it.
9. **During the service.** I keep refining EQ and cleaning up each voice. I use as little compression as possible on vocals, because I want them to sound natural *and* clean.

That's the perfect scenario. It doesn't always go like that.

## When it all goes wrong

Small problems happen almost every week: a monitor that only plays keys even though the routing looks right, a mic that suddenly cuts out, or a buzzing sound on a channel. The real skill isn't avoiding every problem. It's **staying calm, handling the team's requests one at a time, and protecting the service.** In the end, the goal is that the congregation can worship without distractions.

Two moments stand out.

### The wireless mics that turned into telephones

One service, every vocalist was on a wireless mic. Suddenly they all started crackling and cutting in and out, and the singers sounded like they were on a phone call. People in the church stopped focusing on the worship and started looking back at us at the desk.

The band was already playing, so there was no time to troubleshoot. We swapped everyone to wired mics on the spot and sound-checked them live, with no preparation.

Shure lists RF interference, frequency conflicts and antenna placement as the most common causes of wireless dropouts ([Shure's 10-step troubleshooting guide](https://www.shure.com/en-gb/insights/troubleshooting-wireless-dropouts-10-simple-steps)).

### The power cut

Another time, the power went out while we were setting up. Workers outside had damaged the underground cables to the church, and there was no quick fix.

So we went fully acoustic. We placed a piano in the middle of the church and gathered everyone around it, including the person bringing the word, so everyone could hear.

Meanwhile, my team and I kept setting up the whole system anyway, so we'd be ready the moment the power came back. When the lights suddenly turned on mid-worship, we sound-checked everything **muted, through headphones**, so the congregation didn't hear a thing. Then, as people slowly moved from the middle back to their seats, we brought the speakers and subs up step by step.

It worked perfectly, and I honestly think it only worked because of all the trial and error I'd been through before.

## My philosophy: keep it simple

Working in two churches with different gear, rooms and teams taught me one rule: **keep your setup as simple as possible.**

The trap many people fall into is making everything as refined and advanced as they can. But when something breaks, they end up falling back to the basics anyway. If your setup is already simple, troubleshooting is faster, and you can adapt to any room or desk.

And if you're teaching someone? Keep it simple, but **throw them in the deep end** instead of letting them dabble. That's what happened to me, and it's why I learned so much in such a short time.

## Key takeaways

- **Learn the basics first:** gain, faders, basic EQ, cables, and the difference between front of house and monitors.
- **Digital desks help beginners see** what EQ, gates and compression are doing.
- **Use a fixed sound check order:** vocals solo, vocals together, instruments solo, instruments together, full band.
- **Always have a plan B:** wired mics ready, and a way to run the service without power.
- **Simple setups survive problems.** Complex ones collapse into the basics anyway.
